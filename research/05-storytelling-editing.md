### 1.2 Hook patterns [Interpretation from the table]
1. **Second 0–1:** both channels open on *narration over the character already on screen*. There is no logo, intro or title card in any of the 30 strips. The first word lands at ≤ 0.3 s in every video.
2. **The body-anxiety cold open:** "Monkey wake up. Notice something wrong. No [morning wood / hard morning banana]." It appears on **three of the five biggest transcribed videos across both channels**. It works because the viewer recognises the fear within 3 s, the euphemism makes it shareable, and the problem is posed as a mystery ("something wrong").
3. **Delayed promise:** on the **five** biggest transcribed videos (1.42M–1.57M, plus A's 784K "Gooning"), the explicit "today monkey teach you…" lands at **40–66 s**. The gap is filled by a skit, a poem or a story. The next tier promises sooner (Morning routine 27 s, 10x T ~8 s, Skinny Fat 7 s), and weaker videos mostly start teaching within 0–25 s. One counter-example ("…tired of procrastinating", 65 s, 12K) shows that a delayed promise alone isn't enough: the story has to be *funny or emotionally specific*.
4. **Scenario specificity:** B's best openers name a precise, embarrassing moment ("post nut clarity", "exams coming up way too fast… explain to parents"). Generic openers ("Monkey have a lot of work to do") underperform.
5. **Visual hook:** the first frame always shows the gorilla in a *state*: asleep in bed (Increase T, Morning routine, Quit gooning), crowned on a pedestal (Gooning A), seated and tired (Self-educated). There's no static title screen.

## 2. Storytelling structures

### 2.1 Channel A — "Checklist with a comic frame"
```
0:00  Problem image on the gorilla (insecurity)            ← 5–10 s
0:10  [Hits only] Skit / poem / scam-seller / mock epic     ← 20–55 s
~0:40 Promise: "So today, ape teach you how to…"
      Section 1 … Section N   (4–8 handwritten headers; each = mechanism + instruction + 1 gag)
~2:30 Sponsor read (Cal AI) or merch pitch, 50–65 s        ← inserted mid-list
      Remaining sections
~4:00 Recap list ("sleep, eat clean, lift, reduce stress")
      Catchphrase payoff: "…look at the mirror and say 'Wow, that banana is bananas.'"
```
**Archetype:** list explainer (problem → solution list). No conflict arc beyond the opening problem. Humour sits *inside* each list item.

### 2.2 Channel B — "Story → mechanism → protocol → emotional close"
```
0:00  Monkey in a specific failure scene (wake up / exam / supermarket / post-nut)   ← 15–40 s
~0:30 Stakes: "with every extra day, Monkey loses a little more of himself"
~0:45 Promise: "So, today, Monkey will learn three powerful steps…"
      Part 1: a model of the brain (Morning/Evening Monkey; reward-prediction error; rat in the limbic system)
      Part 2: a numbered protocol (Step 1: leave the room in <5 s; Step 2: 10-min timer…)
      [App card or narrated plug, 2:00–3:30, framed as the tool for the protocol]
      Part 3: a twist or "why it gets worse before it gets better" ("the pit of despair")
~end  Emotional close (imagine your future / some days you win, some days you lose)
      "Stay Monke."  + armchair end card
```
**Archetype:** a hero's mini-journey with the viewer as Monkey: problem → understanding → tools → setback → resolve. Explicit open loops are rare. The clearest is in Quit gooning: "Next, Monkey will learn why when he quits gooning, instead of feeling better, he actually ends up feeling more anxious" ([3:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Qg1nFGl-YZc&t=228)). Usually the *story question* posed in the cold open carries the retention. The mechanism is delivered through **parables**: the banana-tree/lemon story, the cookie/radish experiment, rats choosing cocaine.

**Timing [Estimate, from 2-s frames + word times]:** most gags resolve inside **one visual hold (2–4 s)**. Spoken punchline and visual change line up within one sample. Longer set-pieces (skit, poem, experiment) run 10–55 s and sit at the open or at the middle of the video. **Do narration and animation work together?** Yes. In both channels the visual is usually a *literal* illustration of the line, and the joke often lives in the literalness: the pile of molecules in bed; the lemon after "yellow fruit means yummy sweetness".

## 5. Pacing [Measured]

| Metric | A | B |
|---|---|---|
| Median WPM (range) | **172** (128–190) | **164.5** (127–201) |
| WPM on the 3 biggest transcribed hits | 128 (Increase T), 132 (Gooning) | 127 (High T), 170 (Quit gooning), 157 (Self-educated) |
| Avg hold per visual state (2-s-sampled videos) | ≈ 2.7–3.1 s | ≈ 2.2–2.5 s (2026), 3.4–3.9 s (2025) |
| "Monkey/Ape" mentions per minute | 9–16 | 5–18 (falls in Phase 2 as he/him replaces "monkey") |
| Speech coverage | Narration runs almost wall-to-wall (last word within 6 s of the end) | Same |
| Variation | Fast lists, slower skits | Faster middles, slower emotional closes (Self-educated 7:00–8:10) |

**Finding:** the three most-viewed transcribed videos with *skits or dialogue* (A Increase T, A Gooning, B High T) run at ~127–132 WPM, roughly 25% slower than each channel's median. The likely cause [Interpretation] is that skit dialogue and pauses dilute word density, and those pauses are where the jokes land. Sample size is small (3), so treat this as a hypothesis.
